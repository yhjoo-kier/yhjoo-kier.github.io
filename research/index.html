02Research

Codesign of Energy Systems

A long-form view of the thesis, the two tracks, and the projects where they meet.

01The Codesign thesis

Equipment design and operational control are usually treated as two problems. They are one problem.

A heat exchanger is geometrically optimal under a single nominal load. The plant it sits in almost never runs at that load. Most industrial energy systems spend their lives somewhere between off-design points, and the supervisory layer above the equipment spends its life compensating for choices that were locked in years earlier — usually by a different team, often working from a single steady-state spec sheet.

The seam between “what to build” and “how to run it” is where most of the achievable energy savings disappear. A topology-optimized geometry can be undone by a poorly tuned controller. A skilled MPC can only ever recover a fraction of what a mis-sized component leaves on the table. Treating either problem in isolation is locally rational and globally wasteful.

Codesign is the alternative I work on: equipment geometry, supervisory control policy, and the model that links them, optimized against the same objective from the start. It is not a single algorithm — it is a stance about where the design boundary should sit. The two research tracks below are how I explore it.

02Track A — Design optimization

What to build

Track A is the long-running, geometry-side line of work: topology optimization of thermal systems — heat sinks, heat exchangers, thermal insulators — under natural and forced convection, conjugate heat transfer, and multi-objective thermal-structural coupling. The published record on this track goes back to 2015 and is the foundation of the lab’s thermal-design competence.

This track is now advanced primarily through student collaboration. New geometric explorations — aerogel heat sinks, dual-fluid printed-circuit heat exchangers, thermoelectric modules with phase-change buffers — are led by graduate students and supervised against the larger Codesign objective: not “what is the lowest thermal resistance”, but “what geometry leaves the operating policy the most room to run efficiently across the realistic load distribution.”

Methodologically the track contributes the optimization side of the program: density-based topology optimization, shape-dependent convection modeling, deep-learned finite elements, and graph-based convergence acceleration for unstructured meshes. The publications page lists this work under Thermal Design.

03Track B — Operational control

How to run it

Track B is the rapidly growing, operations-side line of work: supervisory control of industrial HVAC and energy-intensive manufacturing facilities. The unit of optimization is the plant in motion — transient loads, set-point schedules, quality and safety constraints, and the human operators in the loop.

The track has moved up a ladder of controller capability. Each rung buys something the rung below it could not represent.

Step 01 · Reactive

PID

Cheap, robust, ubiquitous on the plant floor. Cannot anticipate load, cannot trade objectives, cannot encode constraints — but every operator already trusts it. The realistic baseline.

Step 02 · Predictive

MPC

Model-predictive control buys anticipation and explicit constraint handling. Performance is bounded by the model’s fidelity to the actual plant; for a desiccant-rotor HVAC calibrated against field data, this rung delivered roughly 20% energy savings in our digital-twin study.

Step 03 · Reasoning

AI agent

An LLM-based agent reads plant documentation, reasons over heterogeneous signals, and drives equipment through an ontology layer down to TCP / UDP and the PLC. Hard constraints stay in the lower control layer; the agent handles the parts of supervisory control that previously required an experienced engineer.

The agent rung is what the program is currently spending most of its time on, in collaboration with battery-manufacturing partners. The interesting questions are not “can the agent talk to the PLC” — that is a plumbing problem — but auditability, constraint enforcement under uncertainty, and the right division of labor between the agent, the MPC underneath it, and the human operator above it. The publications page lists this work under Manufacturing Energy and AI / ML for Engineering.

04Where they meet

Codesign in practice

The two tracks come together when a project is allowed to vary both geometry and policy. The current portfolio has two ongoing examples of this and several more on the runway.

Active · Battery manufacturing

LLM agent × HVAC topology

A dry-room HVAC system whose air-handling geometry and supervisory control are jointly evaluated against the same year-of-operation objective. The agent layer determines what the hardware actually has to do; the geometry layer is sized for that, not for a conservative steady-state spec.

Active · Desiccant rotor

Digital twin × control policy

A Simulink digital twin of a desiccant-rotor HVAC system, used both as a controller-design environment and as a feedback signal for component-level redesign decisions. Roughly 20% energy savings against the as-built operating policy in the current study.

On the runway: codesign of electrode-drying lines (where transient model-switching matters more than steady-state efficiency), and topology-optimized heat exchangers whose channel layout is conditioned on the supervisory policy that will run them. The pattern is the same in each case — let the operating policy and the geometry inform each other from the start.
