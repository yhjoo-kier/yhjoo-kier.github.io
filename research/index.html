02Research

Codesign of Energy Systems

A long-form view of the thesis, the two tracks, and the projects where they meet.

01The Codesign thesis

Equipment design, operating policy, and the physics-based model that links them are usually treated as three separate problems. They are one problem.

A heat exchanger is geometrically optimal under a single nominal load. The plant it sits in almost never runs at that load. Most industrial energy systems spend their lives somewhere between off-design points, and the supervisory layer above the equipment spends its life compensating for choices that were locked in years earlier — usually by a different team, often working from a single steady-state spec sheet.

The seams between “what to build”, “how to run it”, and “how it really behaves” are where most of the achievable energy savings disappear. A topology-optimized geometry can be undone by a poorly tuned controller. A skilled MPC can only ever recover a fraction of what a mis-sized component leaves on the table. A digital twin that does not reproduce transient behavior will mislead both of them. Treating any one of these in isolation is locally rational and globally wasteful.

Codesign is the alternative I work on: equipment geometry, supervisory control policy, and the physics-based digital twin that connects them — all three optimized against the same objective from the start. It is not a single algorithm; it is a stance about where the design boundary should sit. The three research tracks below are how I explore it.

02Track A — Design optimization

What to build

Track A is the long-running, geometry-side line of work: topology optimization of thermal systems — heat sinks, heat exchangers, thermal insulators — under natural and forced convection, conjugate heat transfer, and multi-objective thermal-structural coupling. The published record on this track goes back to 2015 and is the foundation of the lab’s thermal-design competence.

This track is now advanced primarily through student collaboration. New geometric explorations — aerogel heat sinks, dual-fluid printed-circuit heat exchangers, thermoelectric modules with phase-change buffers — are led by graduate students and supervised against the larger Codesign objective: not “what is the lowest thermal resistance”, but “what geometry leaves the operating policy the most room to run efficiently across the realistic load distribution” — evaluated against the physics-based twin of the target plant (Track C), not a single steady-state spec sheet.

Methodologically the track contributes the optimization side of the program: density-based topology optimization, shape-dependent convection modeling, deep-learned finite elements, and graph-based convergence acceleration for unstructured meshes. The publications page lists this work under Thermal Design.

03Track B — Operational control

How to run it

Track B is the rapidly growing, operations-side line of work: supervisory control of industrial HVAC and energy-intensive manufacturing facilities. The unit of optimization is the plant in motion — transient loads, set-point schedules, quality and safety constraints, and the human operators in the loop.

The track has moved up a ladder of controller capability. Each rung buys something the rung below it could not represent.

Step 01 · Reactive

PID

Cheap, robust, ubiquitous on the plant floor. Cannot anticipate load, cannot trade objectives, cannot encode constraints — but every operator already trusts it. The realistic baseline.

Step 02 · Predictive

MPC

Model-predictive control buys anticipation and explicit constraint handling. Performance is bounded by the model’s fidelity to the actual plant; for a desiccant-rotor HVAC calibrated against field data, this rung delivered roughly 20% energy savings in our digital-twin study.

Step 03 · Reasoning

AI agent

An LLM-based agent reads plant documentation, reasons over heterogeneous signals, and drives equipment through an ontology layer down to TCP / UDP and the PLC. Hard constraints stay in the lower control layer; the agent handles the parts of supervisory control that previously required an experienced engineer.

The agent rung is what the program is currently spending most of its time on, in collaboration with battery-manufacturing partners. Each rung — PID, MPC, and the agent — is designed and stress-tested against the physics-based digital twin of the target plant (Track C) before any of it touches hardware. The interesting questions are not “can the agent talk to the PLC” — that is a plumbing problem — but auditability, constraint enforcement under uncertainty, and the right division of labor between the agent, the MPC underneath it, and the human operator above it. The publications page lists this work under Manufacturing Energy and AI / ML for Engineering.

04Track C — The model in between

The substrate Codesign runs on

Codesign needs a model that is honest about both axes. A CAD file does not tell you how a year of operation will play out. A historian database does not tell you what a different geometry would have done. Neither one supports a fair argument across the seam between design and operation.

Track C is the work that sits between them: physics-based digital twins of the target plant — first-principles models built in Simulink and Modelica, CFD-derived reduced-order models, and multiscale couplings between component-level dynamics and process-level mass/energy balances. The twins are calibrated against field measurements and held to a standard most simulations are not: they must respond honestly to both a change in geometry and a change in supervisory policy. A twin that only gets the steady state right is not enough for Codesign.

Concretely, the desiccant-rotor twin sits here. The multiscale electrode-drying model with state-dependent transient model switching sits here. Each one serves a double role: testbed for the operating policy (the environment Track B controllers learn and are validated against) and feedback signal for component-level redesign (the evidence a Track A geometry change is worth making). The same model is the substrate both tracks share.

The methodological questions are model fidelity versus tractability, calibration discipline (what does it mean for a twin to be “good enough” for a codesign decision), and the right interface between a high-fidelity twin and the surrogate models that the inner optimization loops actually consume. Some of this work appears under Manufacturing Energy on the publications page; some is moving from digital twin into deployable supervisory control on real lines.

05Where they meet

Codesign in practice

The three tracks come together when a project is allowed to vary all three at once — geometry, operating policy, and the model that links them. The current portfolio has two ongoing examples and several more on the runway.

Active · Battery manufacturing

LLM agent × HVAC topology

A dry-room HVAC system whose air-handling geometry, supervisory control, and physics-based twin are co-developed against the same year-of-operation objective. The twin determines what the hardware actually has to do under realistic load; the geometry is sized for that, not for a conservative steady-state spec; the agent is tuned against the same model and never sees hardware until it has passed the twin.

Active · Desiccant rotor

Digital twin × control policy

A Simulink digital twin of a desiccant-rotor HVAC system used as the shared substrate for both controller design and component-level redesign decisions. Roughly 20% energy savings against the as-built operating policy in the current study — the same number Track A and Track B each get to draw on when arguing for a change.

On the runway: codesign of electrode-drying lines, where the transient model-switching done in the twin (Track C) drives both dryer-zone redesign (Track A) and the drying-schedule controller (Track B); and topology-optimized heat exchangers whose channel layout is conditioned on the supervisory policy that will run them. The pattern is the same in each case — the twin is the shared arena where geometry and policy argue with each other before anything is built.
